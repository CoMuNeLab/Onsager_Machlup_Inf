\begin{abstract}
\vspace{0.2 cm}  
Recovering the interaction structure of a complex stochastic dynamical system from time-series observations is an inherently ill-posed problem: the posterior over network configurations is high-dimensional, multimodal, and shaped by the non-equilibrium nature of the available data. We propose a Bayesian ensemble inference framework grounded in the Onsager-Machlup path probability formalism, which provides a rigorous likelihood for SDE-governed trajectories without requiring stationarity, and is coupled with a node-wise block-coordinate Metropolis-Hastings sampler that exploits the factorization of the path probability to make inference tractable. A evidence-estimation-based model comparison scheme jointly recovers the dynamical parameters alongside the network topology. Applying the framework to gene regulatory network (GRN) inference, we demonstrate on synthetic data that ensemble-based posterior analysis produce more reliable results, in particular when considering perturbation predictions and network topological statistics, than single-point estimation. The framework is general and can be applied to any mechanistic SDE model, offering a powerful tool for uncertainty-aware inference in complex stochastic systems.
\vspace{0.2 cm}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

% network inference is important
Inferring the structure of a complex dynamical system from time-series observations is a fundamental challenge in the physical, biological, and data sciences~\cite{newman2018network,peixoto2025network,nitzan2017revealing,timme2007revealing, brunton2016discovering}. 
%%% \valeria{aggiungerei altre referenze, ad esempio (non necessariamente in quest'ordine):}
%%% Aggiunti: Peixoto PRX 2025, Newman NatPhys 2018, Barzel NatPhys 2013, Peel NatComm 2022, Nitzan SciAdv 2017, Timme 2007
%%% Guimerà PNAS 2009: data are referred to edges

% in particular GRN inference is important
In particular, inferring the structure of gene regulatory networks (GRNs) from gene expression is a crucial problem in systems biology, with important implications for understanding cellular function and disease mechanisms~\cite{karlebach2008modelling, barabasi2011network}. Knowing the structure of the GRN allows us to predict how the system will respond to perturbations such as gene knockouts or drug treatments, and to identify potential therapeutic targets. 
% structure and dynamics are intertwined
However, structure and dynamics are deeply intertwined in complex systems~\cite{gross2008adaptive,barzel2013universality,ghavasieh2024diversity,strogatz2024nonlinear,barzon2024unraveling,sole2001complexity,de2026decoding}, and thus inferring the network structure without explicitly modeling the underlying dynamics can lead to inaccurate and unreliable results. %chiedere a manlio per la referenza
% paramter non-identifiability and structural non-identifiability
When the system is governed by nonlinear, stochastic dynamics, and the observations are noisy and limited in time, the problem becomes particularly severe: multiple distinct configurations of the interactions can produce trajectories that are statistically indistinguishable from the data~\cite{villaverde2014reverse}. These difficulties, broadly referred to as structural non-identifiability, call for inference approaches that go beyond point estimation, instead quantifying and propagating uncertainty over the full space of plausible model configurations.
% likelihood discontinuity and network emergence
Another important factor is that when dealing with networks, even small changes in the parameters can lead to large changes in the system's behavior, due to the emergence of collective phenomena and critical transitions~\cite{boccaletti2006complex,dorogovtsev2008critical,pastor2015epidemic, strogatz2024nonlinear}. This leads to highly discontinuous likelihood landscapes, where the maxima are isolated and separated by low-probability barriers. For this reason, in most of the cases, standard incremental optimization methods are not good solutions for such problems.
%%% \valeria{altri paper eventalmente da citare: , Pastor Satorras RMP 2015,...}
%%%Aggiunti: Boccaletti Phys Rep 2006,  Dorogovtsev 2008 RMP
% importance of bayesian inference and uncertainty quantification
A natural and principled route to this goal is Bayesian inference, in which a posterior distribution over model parameters is obtained by combining a likelihood function for the observed data with prior knowledge about the system~\cite{peixoto2019network, newman2018network,raimondo2021measuring,peel2022statistical,peixoto2025network}. This approach allows us to quantify uncertainty over the inferred interactions, which is crucial for guiding experimental validation and for making reliable predictions about the system's behavior.

% existing methods
%% correlation based methods
Most of the methodologies proposed to tackle this problem rely on statistical associations between observed variables, such as correlation or mutual information, to infer the presence of interactions between them~\cite{margolin2006aracne,marbach2012wisdom, marku2023time}. Additionally, they do not provide measures of uncertainty over the inferred interactions, which is crucial for guiding experimental validation and for making reliable predictions about the system's behavior. 
%% bayesian structure learning and graphical models, DAGs
Other methods attempt to learn the posterior distribution over network configurations using Bayesian structure learning techniques, often based on graphical models and directed acyclic graphs (DAGs)~\cite{glymour2019review}. These methods can provide uncertainty estimates, but they typically assume that the underlying system is governed by a DAG structure, which is not always valid in real systems, and thus they cannot properly capture cyclic interactions, which are common in biological systems. Additionally, they often rely on assumptions of stationarity and equilibrium, which are not always valid in real systems, and thus they cannot fully exploit the information contained in non-equilibrium trajectories, which are often the only ones available in real experiments.
%% cyclic structure learning methods
Given the inherently cyclic nature of gene regulatory systems, it is crucial to consider methods that can capture such interactions. Multiple approaches have been proposed to solve the issue in different fields~\cite{huang2020causal,runge2019detecting}. However, these methods have limitations, such as single point estimation, lack of uncertainty quantification, and reliance on assumptions that may not hold in real systems, such as stationarity and equilibrium.
%% work atanokvic
A recent approach~\cite{atanackovic2023dyngfn} addresses some of these limitations by proposing a Bayesian framework for inferring cyclic interactions from time-series data by modeling the system's latent drift function through either linear solvers or non-linear MLPs. While this method represents a significant step forward, it still does not explicitly define the mechanistic SDE model underlying the dynamics, and thus remains of difficult interpretation, comparison with other mechanistic models of gene regulation and with the risk of data overfitting. Additionally, it does not provide a principled way to select the dynamical parameters of the SDE model, such as noise amplitude and relaxation rates.

% Our proposal  
In this work, we propose a mechanistic, ensemble-based Bayesian framework for GRN inference from non-equilibrium time-series. We formulate a dynamical likelihood for trajectories governed by non-linear Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs), and integrate it with the non-parametric Minimum Description Length (MDL) structural prior and Stochastic Block Model (SBM) developed by Peixoto \cite{peixoto2019network, peixoto2025network}. This joint structural-dynamical optimization is powered by a highly scalable block-coordinate Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampler.

Crucially, our method does not rely on assumptions of stationarity, enabling the full exploitation of transient trajectories which contain richer causal information. We demonstrate that the fundamental challenge of structural non-identifiability can be overcome by globally constraining kinetic parameters, which forces the inference engine to explain heterogeneous biological fluctuations exclusively through the inferred topology rather than absorbing variance via localized parameter overfitting. By navigating the multimodal posterior landscape, our method recovers a rigorous probabilistic ensemble of biologically plausible networks. We show that this ensemble-based approach significantly outperforms state-of-the-art point-estimates, providing robust and reliable predictions for downstream perturbation analysis~\cite{peel2022statistical}.

% Applications
%% Syntetic network on nonlinear SDE model
As a primary application, we start from a nonlinear SDE model with sigmoidal activation functions -- capturing saturation, oscillatory, and noise-driven regimes -- and then we perform inference on synthetic networks. We show that the ensemble approach provides a more reliable reconstruction of the true network topology than single-point estimation, in particular for the prediction of gene knockout effects. We also demonstrate how incorporating sparse prior knowledge about network connectivity and known regulatory interactions improves inference in data-limited regimes.

\section{Bayesian Network Inference}

%Paragraph 1: The Inverse Problem. Formulate network reconstruction as an inverse problem. Introduce Bayes' Theorem.




When trying to approach the inverse problem of network reconstruction from some data $\mathbf{X}$, the most rigorous and transparent approach is using Bayesian inference:

\begin{equation}
    P(\mathbf{W},\theta|\mathbf{X}) = \frac{P(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{W}, \theta)P(\mathbf{W}, \theta)}{P(\mathbf{X})}
\end{equation}


%Paragraph 2: Components of Bayes. Explain the Likelihood (how well the network explains the trajectory) and the Prior (our biological assumptions, e.g., sparsity).
Using this equation, we suppose that the data we have are generated by a specific generative model, described by the likelihood function $P(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{W}, \theta)$, where $\mathbf{W}$ is the encodes the network structure and weights and $\theta$ are the extra parameters of the generative model. 
In this work we will focus on data organized in time series, so, if we have a network of $N$ nodes, and the data are organized in $M$ time points, we can write the data as a matrix $\mathbf{X} = \{\mathbf{x}_{t_1}, \mathbf{x}_{t_2}, ..., \mathbf{x}_{t_M}\}$ of size $M \times N$, where $\mathbf{x}_m$ is a vector of size $N$ that encodes the state of the nodes at time $t_m$. In this case, the likelihood function can be factorized as:

\begin{equation}
    P(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{W}, \theta) = \prod_{m=1}^{M-1} P(\mathbf{x}_{t_m+\Delta t}|\mathbf{x}_{t_m}, \mathbf{W}, \theta)
    \label{eq:general_likelihood}
\end{equation}

In most of the realistic scenarios, the data we have are scarce and noisy, and the generative models are complex and high-dimensional. For this reasons, when we try to perform inference on the generative model, we can encounter two main problems: non-identifiability and overfitting. Non-identifiability occurs when multiple distinct configurations of the interactions can produce trajectories that are statistically indistinguishable from the data. Overfitting occurs when the model is too complex and fits the noise in the data rather than the underlying signal. 

To overcome these issues we need to incorporate an extra term which acts as a regularizer, penalizing complex models and incorporating prior knowledge about the system. This term is the prior distribution $P(\mathbf{W}, \theta)$, that enables the inference process to be guided towards more plausible configurations, and to avoid overfitting.


%Paragraph 3: MDL and Compression. Frame Bayesian optimization conceptually: finding the network that maximizes Minimum Description Length (MDL) compression of the data.

One way to interpret the Bayesian inference framework is through the lens of Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle. Taking the logarithm of Bayes' theorem, we can rewrite it as:

\begin{equation}
    \log P(\mathbf{W},\theta|\mathbf{X}) = \log P(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{W}, \theta) + \log P(\mathbf{W}, \theta) - \log P(\mathbf{X})
\end{equation}

In this way the inference problem can be seen as a maximum compression problem, where we want to find the network configuration that requires the least amount of information to describe the data. This is a crucial aspect of Bayesian inference, as it clearly states the goal of the inference process: to find the network configuration that best explains the data while being as simple as possible.

%Paragraph 4: The Paradigm Shift. Contrast finding the single "best" network (MAP estimate) with generating a probabilistic ensemble. Why ensembles are necessary when single false-positive edges can ruin biological interpretations.

Once we have defined both the generative model and the prior distribution, we can find the configuration that is able to best compress the data, which is called the Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimate. However, in many cases, the MAP estimate is not sufficient to capture the full complexity of the inference problem. In particular, the best network configuration often does not correspond exactly to the true underlying network, due to data scarcity, noise and other aspects that cannot be completely comptured by the generative model. 

This aspect is particularly important in context where the mis-specification of a single edge can have a relevant impact on the subsequent system's analysis. This is particulary relevant in the context of gene regulatory networks, where in most of the case, the inferred network is used to perform perturbation analysis, and the mis-specification of a single edge can lead to completely wrong predictions. 

For this reason, it is crucial to consider the full posterior distribution over network configurations, rather than just the MAP estimate. This allows us to capture the uncertainty in the inference process and to generate a probabilistic ensemble of plausible network configurations, which can be used to perform more robust and reliable analyses. This can be acheived by sampling from the posterior distribution using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, which allow us to generate a representative set of network configurations that are consistent with the generative model and the model's constraints.


\subsection{Capturing Saturation and Relaxation in Networked Systems}


%Paragraph 1: The SDE Model. Present the specific SDE: continuous exponential decay, saturating non-linear activation (sigmoid), and the interaction matrix W. Crucially, explain why we chose this model: It is the "simplest non-trivial model" capable of capturing the universal features of bounded complex networks—namely, intrinsic exponential relaxation (without which states would blow up) and bounded non-linear coupling (because real-world networks exert saturating, not infinite, influence).

As a generative model for the data, we consider a system of stochastic differential equations (SDEs) that describe the dynamics of a network of interacting nodes. This is the most general and flexible framework for modeling complex systems, as it allows us to capture both the deterministic dynamics of the system and the intrinsic stochasticity that characterizes the aquired data. 
As a representative case study, we focus on the following model:

\begin{equation}
    dx_i = -\gamma \, x_i + V \sigma \, \Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{N} W_{ij}\, x_j \Bigg) +  \sqrt{D \, x_i} \, dW_i(t)
\end{equation}

Altough simple, this model is able to capture the universal features of bounded complex networks, namely, intrinsic exponential relaxation and bounded non-linear coupling. Such features are common to many real-world systems, including brain networks, ecological systems, macroeconomics and many others. A particular example of such systems is represented by gene regulatory networks, where the nodes represent genes and the edges represent regulatory interactions between them. In this context, the exponential decay term represents the natural degradation of gene products, while the saturating non-linear activation term represents the limited capacity of genes to influence each other. The interaction matrix $W$ encodes the strength and directionality of these interactions, and the multiplicative noise term captures the intrinsic stochasticity that characterizes gene expression. For the following, we will use as a interpretative framework the context of gene regulatory networks.

In this kind of systems, the correct inference of the interaction matrix $W$ is crucial for understanding the underlying regulatory mechanisms and for making predictions about the system's behavior under different conditions, and for this reason, the Bayesian inference framework is particularly well-suited for this task, as it allows to produce a probabilistic ensemble of plausible network configurations that can be used to perform more robust and reliable analyses.


%Paragraph 2: Multiplicative Noise. Argue the choice of Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) multiplicative noise over additive noise, tying it to realistic biological burstiness.

A particular aspect of the model that we consider is the choice of the multiplicative noise term, which is modeled as a Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) process. This choice is motivated by the fact that gene expression is known to be a bursty process, where the production of gene products occurs in short bursts rather than at a constant rate. This burstiness can be captured by the multiplicative noise term, which allows for fluctuations in the gene expression levels that are proportional to the current state of the system. In contrast, additive noise would not capture this feature, as it would introduce fluctuations that are independent of the current state of the system.

\subsection{Theoretical Validation: Exact Transition Moments}

%Paragraph 2: Theoretical Flaws of E-M. Explain why E-M is mathematically expected to fail for large sampling intervals (tangent overshoots, unbounded variance).

While computationally trivial, the Euler-Maruyama linear approximation is mathematically guaranteed to become unstable at large measurement intervals ($\Delta t$), which are common in real-world transcriptomic time-series (e.g., hours between sampling). Under large $\Delta t$, the linear tangent completely ignores the exponential relaxation inherent to the $-\gamma x_t$ degradation term. If the degradation rate is fast ($\gamma \Delta t > 1$), this results in severe mathematical overshoot, where the predicted mean may oscillate and drop below zero. Furthermore, EM assumes the variance grows linearly without bound ($2D x_t \Delta t$), ignoring the variance saturation caused by mean-reverting dynamics.

%Paragraph 3: The Exact Transition Moments. Introduce the Zero-Order Hold assumption to derive the exact CIR moments.

To construct a theoretical stress-test and validate the robustness of this discretization, we introduce an exact analytical integration approach predicated on a zero-order hold assumption. We assume that the aggregate regulatory input from the rest of the network, $m_i(t)$, remains piecewise constant over the short interval $[t, t+\Delta t]$. Under this assumption, the non-linear coupling term $V \, \sigma(m_i(t))$ simplifies to a constant scalar $b$.

The governing SDE for a single gene isolated during this time step reduces to a strictly linear stochastic differential equation:

\[ dx_t = (-\gamma x_t + b) dt + \sqrt{2D x_t} dW_t \]

By isolating the gene's state variable $x_t$, the SDE is mathematically equivalent to a continuous CIR process, which permits an exact analytical solution for its transition moments regardless of the magnitude of $\Delta t$. 

%Paragraph 4: Theoretical Bounds. Report the exact mean and exact variance (via Itô's Isometry).
    
By defining the deterministic steady-state asymptote as $\mu_\infty = \frac{V \, \sigma(m_i(t))}{\gamma}$, the exact expected mean is strictly bounded by the exponential relaxation:

\[ \mu(t+\Delta t) = \mu_\infty + \big(x_i(t) - \mu_\infty\big)e^{-\gamma_i \Delta t} \]

And the exact integrated variance accumulated over the interval $\Delta t$ correctly saturates to a finite limit:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    \Sigma(t+\Delta t) &= \frac{2D_i}{\gamma_i}\big(x_i(t) - \mu_\infty\big)\Big(e^{-\gamma_i \Delta t} - e^{-2\gamma_i \Delta t}\Big) \\
    &\quad + \frac{D_i \mu_\infty}{\gamma_i}\Big(1 - e^{-2\gamma_i \Delta t}\Big)
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The full analytical derivation of these moments via Itô's Isometry is provided in the Supplementary Material.

\subsection{Inference engine}

%Paragraph 1: Graph-Tool Framework. Present Tiago Peixoto’s perspective on network inference (stochastic block models, non-parametric priors).

Once the generative model is defined, we employ an extension of the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) inference engine developed by Peixoto~\cite{peixoto2025}, implemented via the \textit{graph-tool} library. 
This method incorporates a hierarchical, non-parametric Minimum Description Length (MDL) prior, which goal is to rigorously penalize model complexity without relying on weight shrinkage. It achieves this via weight quantization: rather than treating every non-zero edge as having an infinitely precise, unique weight (which would require infinite information to describe), the algorithm is forced to compress the network's continuous weights into a highly redundant, finite vocabulary of discrete categories. 

This adaptive compression is formalized by marginalizing the prior over several latent structural and parametric variables:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        P(\mathbf{W} \mid \lambda, \Delta) &= \sum_{z,K,\mathbf{A},E} P(\mathbf{W} \mid z, \mathbf{A})\, P(z \mid \lambda, \Delta, K)\, \\
        &\quad\times P(K \mid \mathbf{A})\,P(\mathbf{A} \mid E)\,P(E)
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:mdl_prior}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{W}$ represents the continuous interaction weights of the network, and $\mathbf{A}$ is its corresponding binary adjacency matrix containing exactly $E$ non-zero edges. The parameter $K$ denotes the total number of unique, discrete weight categories into which the edges are grouped, while $z = \{z_1, \dots, z_K\}$ represents the set of continuous values for those $K$ categories.

This hierarchical formulation allows the inference engine to dynamically adjust the complexity of the model. Several of the marginal distributions represent simple uniform priors that serve as baseline complexity penalties: $P(E)$ is uniformly distributed over the possible number of edges and $P(K \mid \mathbf{A})$ is uniform over the number of discrete weight categories ($K \le E$).

The term responsible for the sparse structure of the network is:

\begin{equation} 
    P(\mathbf{A} \mid E) = \frac{\delta_{\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}, E}}{\binom{N^2}{E}} 
\end{equation}

which penalizes topologies with large number of edges, leading to small description length for sparse graphs.

The other two terms govern how the continuous weights are compressed into the discrete vocabulary $z$. The first one, $P(\mathbf{W} \mid z, \mathbf{A})$, quantifies how much the number of paramters categories impact on the compression level of the data.
If $m_k$ represents the number of edges assigned to a specific weight category $k$, this probability distribution yields a multinomial combinatorial term proportional to $\frac{\prod_k m_k!}{E!}$. From an MDL perspective, this term heavily rewards parameter reuse. Because the inverse of the multinomial coefficient maximizes when many edges share the exact same weight category (e.g., $m_k$ is large), the algorithm is strongly incentivized to minimize $K$ and group the weights into a small, highly redundant vocabulary.

The second term, $P(z \mid \lambda, \Delta, K)$, prevents the absolute values of the weight categories from diverging. They are drawn from a quantized Laplace distribution defined by the scale parameter $\lambda$ and a numerical precision $\Delta$, yielding a probability proportional to $e^{-\lambda \sum_{k=1}^K |z_k|}$. This does not yeald to strong shrinkage of the weights, making it a more robust alternative to the common LASSO regularization.

Alongside to these terms, the prior could also include the Stochastic Block Model (SBM) with degree correction, which allows the algorithm to exploit the modular structure of the network to further compress the description length. This is particularly useful in the context of gene regulatory networks, where genes are often organized into functional modules that share similar regulatory patterns, and for the case scenario considered in this work, is able to classify between the master regulators and the downstream targets, improving the overall inference performance.

All these elements compose a non-parametric prior that is able to adaptively penalize model complexity, while still allowing for a rich and flexible representation of the network structure and weights. This is crucial for avoiding overfitting and ensuring that the inferred networks are both biologically plausible and statistically robust.

%Paragraph 2: Integration. Explain how your exact analytical likelihood is embedded into the MCMC engine of graph-tool.

To optimize the joint posterior of the network topology and the global kinetic parameters, we employ a block-coordinate optimization scheme. The algorithm alternates between two phases: first, the discrete topological space (edges and block assignments) is sampled stochastically using Metropolis-Hastings MCMC sweeps. The sampling scheme for the topology is based on the $NN_{Descent}$ algorithm, which is able to solve the combinatorial optimization problem in a subquadratic time complexity of order $O(N\,log^2 N)$, instead of the naive $O(N^2)$ time complexity for standard gradient descent algorithms. On the other hand, the classes of parameters values are obtained by a system of merging and splitting of the weight classes and random bisection to obtain new values for the weight categories.

Second, the global continuous kinetic parameters $\theta = \{\gamma, V, D\}$ are deterministically optimized conditional on the current topology using the Nelder-Mead simplex algorithm to minimize the total description length. This choice was made because the likelihood function is convex with respect to the kinetic parameters, and given a fixed topology and weight categories, the optimization problem is well-posed and can be solved efficiently using a deterministic algorithm.

This hybrid approach allows the engine to efficiently navigate the highly combinatorial structural space while quickly relaxing the continuous dynamics into their local optimum.

\section{Results}

To systematically evaluate the performance of our Bayesian inference framework, we conducted a comprehensive grid search analysis on synthetic, scale-free gene regulatory networks modeled via non-linear SDEs. The experimental setup was designed to explore a wide range of realistic observational conditions by varying the network size ($N$), the number of sampled time-series trajectories ($M_{pairs}$), the sampling interval ($\Delta t$), and the dynamical regime from which the data were collected (transient versus stationary). Unless otherwise specified, the networks were generated as directed scale-free graphs using the Price model to ensure the presence of highly-connected master regulators, and trajectories were simulated under moderate intrinsic noise. The results of this extensive analysis demonstrate the robustness of the ensemble-based approach in recovering the underlying true network topology. (Full details regarding the generative model parameters and integration schemes are provided in the Supplementary Materials).

The structural reconstruction of the gene regulatory network demonstrates a strong dependence on the experimental inference settings, particularly the temporal measurement resolution ($\Delta t$) and the number of available sample pairs ($M$).

While increasing the number of samples ($M$) initially improves structural recovery by providing more data to constrain the inference, we observe a saturation and subsequent overfitting effect for excessively large $M$. In this high-data regime, the True Positive Rate (TPR) remains high, but the False Positive Rate (FPR) begins to increase. This suggests that rather than continuing to generalize the true causal interactions, the model starts overfitting to the accumulated stochastic noise present in the extensive sample pairs. This effect comes directly from the Bayesian framework's inherent trade-off between model complexity and data fit, as encoded in the Minimum Description Length (MDL) prior. As the number of samples increases, the optimizer is incentivized to explain more of the variance in the data, which can lead to the inclusion of spurious edges that do not correspond to genuine interactions.

Furthermore, the inference accuracy is highly sensitive to the measurement interval $\Delta t$, displaying a clear optimal intermediate range. Measurements taken too frequently (very small $\Delta t$) suffer from high correlations between consecutive states and noise domination in the finite-difference derivative estimates. Conversely, excessively large intervals fail to capture the fast underlying interaction dynamics, leading to severe aliasing and degraded structural recovery.

In particular, the grid search revealed that the method performs best when operating on non-equilibrium transient trajectories and when employing the exact analytical transition probability, which successfully mitigates the severe discretization errors that typically plague naive approximations at large sampling intervals. In the following subsections, we delve into the specific insights drawn from these experiments.

\subsection{The necessity of simple models: the problem of overfitting}

In the SDE model we consider (Eq.~1), the dynamical parameters $\theta = \{\gamma, V, D\}$ are global parameters that are shared across all nodes in the network. This means that the inference of these parameters is not node-specific, but rather a global optimization problem that takes into account the entire network's dynamics. 

A fundamental challenge in inferring SDE-driven networks from sparse time-series data is the degeneracy between intrinsic node dynamics and network-driven coupling. If the kinetic parameters ($\gamma, V, D$) are allowed to vary freely for every individual node, the model becomes overparameterized. Under the MDL framework, the optimizer naturally penalizes structural complexity; thus, it preferentially tunes the local intrinsic parameters to absorb the trajectory variance, resulting in an artificial collapse of the network topology (an empty graph).

To break this degeneracy, we constrain the kinetic parameters to be global, shared symmetrically across all nodes in the network. By stripping the nodes of their individual parametric flexibility, we force the intrinsic dynamics to act as a uniform, homogeneous baseline. Consequently, the only mathematical avenue left for the inference engine to explain the diverse, node-specific heterogeneous fluctuations observed in the data is to reconstruct the true underlying interaction topology $W$. This global constraint acts as a powerful structural inducer, guaranteeing that the inferred network is driven by genuine interactions rather than localized parametric overfitting.

In Fig.~\ref{fig:inference_performance}e, we illustrate this phenomenon. When the kinetic parameters are allowed to vary per node, the inferred network collapses to a trivial empty graph, as the optimizer absorbs all variance into the local parameters. In contrast, when the kinetic parameters are constrained globally, the inference engine successfully recovers a rich and biologically plausible network topology.

\subsection{Benchmarking against state-of-the-art inference paradigms}

This kind of model is able to help avoid overfitting also when compared to other state-of-the-art inference models. We performed a comprehensive benchmarking study where our proposed Bayesian stochastic differential equation (SDE) approach was compared against four distinct canonical paradigms: dynGENIE3~\cite{huynh2018dyngenie3} (non-parametric tree-based inference), Sparse ODE via Lasso~\cite{tibshirani1996regression, bonneau2006inferelator} (sparse parametric linear recovery), Granger Causality~\cite{granger1969investigating, shojaie2010discovering} (classical statistical temporal prediction), and PySINDy~\cite{kaptanoglu2021pysindy} (modern data-driven sequential thresholding). Over independent replicates of simulated scale-free networks, we assessed the models on their structural recovery and predictive generalization. 

The results reported in Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarking}b indicated a strong tendency for standard baselines to overfit the highly transient training data. In particular, while dynGENIE3 reached comparable structural recovery performances, it exhibited a significant discrepancy between its training and out-of-sample test prediction errors (e.g., $\Delta \approx 0.040\pm0.005$), indicating that it overfitted the data. Conversely, our Graph-Tool SDE implementation provided robust structural inference (AUPRC = $0.95\pm0.05$) while maintaining almost identical training and test errors ($\Delta \approx 0.004\pm0.004$). This parity indicates strong trajectory compression and out-of-sample generalization, demonstrating that the central advantage of the method lies in the Bayesian framework's inherent capacity to prevent overfitting and accurately recover the underlying causal interactions.

\subsection{Bayesian Topology Inference is Robust to SDE Discretization Errors}

% Present the "Stress Discretization" benchmarking results. Show how the naive E-M approach performances are compatible with the one from the exact model, and thus we can stick to it because it is computationally cheaper.

The Euler-Maruyama (E-M) approximation is a widely used method for discretizing stochastic differential equations (SDEs) due to its simplicity and computational efficiency. However, it is well-known that E-M can introduce significant discretization errors, particularly when the sampling interval $\Delta t$ is large relative to the system's intrinsic relaxation timescales.
To assess the impact of these discretization errors on network inference, we conducted a systematic benchmarking study comparing the performance of our inference engine using both the exact analytical transition moments and the E-M approximation across a range of sampling intervals.
By looking at the results in Fig.~\ref{fig:inference_performance}g, we found that both methods lead to comparable inference performances, even at large $\Delta t$ where E-M is theoretically expected to fail. In particular, we tested the inference engine on both the standard conditions used for the grid search and on stress-test conditions, in one case significantly increasing the degradation rate $\gamma$ forcing a stiff dynamics, and in the other case significantly increasing the noise level $D$ leading to a highly stochastic dynamics. In both cases, the inference engine produced comparable results using either the exact analytical transition moments or the E-M approximation, demonstrating that the inference engine is robust to the discretization errors introduced by E-M.

A possible explanation for this result is that the inference engine is able to compensate for the discretization errors introduced by E-M by adjusting the global kinetic parameters $\theta = \{\gamma, V, D\}$. In particular, the optimizer can tune these parameters to effectively absorb the discrepancies between the E-M approximation and the true dynamics, allowing the inference engine to still recover a plausible network topology.

This suggests that, for the purpose of network inference, the E-M approximation is sufficiently accurate and can be used as a computationally cheaper alternative to the exact analytical method.

\subsection{Working outside stationarity conveys more information}

% Demonstrate that inference relies on transient dynamics. If the GRN is stuck at a steady-state, highly correlated genes are indistinguishable from causal genes. Show that trajectories actively relaxing to steady-state provide the necessary causal information.

Our Bayesian SDE approach is particularly well-suited for analyzing non-equilibrium transient trajectories, which are often the only type of data available in real-world biological experiments. In contrast, many existing methods rely on stationary or equilibrium assumptions, which can lead to misleading inferences when applied to transient data.
To illustrate this, we analyze the performance of our method on synthetic datasets generated from both transient and stationary regimes. The results reported in Fig.~\ref{fig:inference_performance}f show that the inference engine achieves significantly higher accuracy when operating on transient trajectories, as these contain richer causal information about the underlying network structure. This holds for all the network sizes and sampling intervals tested, demonstrating the robustness of the approach across a wide range of conditions.
In contrast, stationary data often lead to sistematically worse results, probabily due to the strong presence of ambiguous correlations that can confound the inference process.

This can be particularly useful in the context of gene regulatory networks, where the system is often perturbed by external stimuli or environmental changes, leading to transient responses that reveal the causal relationships between genes. By leveraging the full information content of these non-equilibrium trajectories, our Bayesian SDE framework is able to recover more accurate and biologically meaningful network topologies.


\subsection{Toward an ensemble perspective}



\paragraph{Probabilistic evaluation of inference results\\}

% Show that the MCMC produces a distribution of networks. Define edge probabilities rather than binary present/absent edges.
Standard network inference methods typically yield a single, optimal point-estimate of the network topology. However, biological systems are heavily under-determined, meaning many distinct network structures can explain the observed data equally well. To address this, we approach the inference problem probabilistically using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling. Rather than forcing binary present/absent edge decisions, the MCMC produces a posterior distribution of viable network topologies. From this ensemble, we can compute marginal edge probabilities and expected interaction weights, effectively shifting the paradigm from finding the ``one true network'' to capturing and quantifying the full structural uncertainty inherent in the data.

Evaluating this probabilistic output requires a departure from traditional binary classification metrics. To assess topological accuracy, standard metrics such as the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) and Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC) were adapted by utilizing the marginal edge probabilities as natural confidence scores. However, evaluating the inferred regulatory weights presents a deeper challenge; due to parameter non-identifiability in non-linear dynamical systems, absolute parameter values often diverge even when the dynamic behavior is perfectly captured. Therefore, we evaluated the quantitative reconstruction by focusing on the relative hierarchy of regulatory interactions. We employed Rank-Biased Overlap (RBO) and Spearman Rank correlation to compare the expected inferred weights against the true network, emphasizing that predicting the correct hierarchy of top master-regulators is biologically more relevant than exact parameter matching. (A comprehensive formulation of these metrics, alongside distributional evaluations using the Wasserstein distance, is detailed in the Supplementary Materials).

\paragraph{Impact on perturbation analysis\\}

% The ultimate biological payoff: demonstrate that predicting the effect of a drug/knockout (perturbation) is highly sensitive to single edges. Show how an ensemble of networks gives a confidence interval for a perturbation outcome, whereas a single MAP network gives a brittle, potentially wrong prediction.


The ultimate biological payoff of this probabilistic framework lies in forecasting the downstream effects of targeted interventions, such as drug treatments or gene knockouts. Predicting perturbation outcomes is highly sensitive to individual false-positive or false-negative edges; a single incorrect regulatory link in a point-estimate model can catastrophically derail the simulated dynamics. To assess the predictive robustness of our approach, we performed an \textit{in silico} perturbation experiment by simulating a gene knockout and evaluating the resulting systemic shift in steady states. We contrasted the predictive power of the full posterior ensemble against the Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimate—the single sampled network with the minimum description length.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarking}c, while the MAP point-estimate generalized poorly to the perturbation due to overfitting the wild-type data ($\rho = 0.800$), marginalizing over topological uncertainty allowed the expected shift of the ensemble to accurately predict the systemic response ($\rho = 0.983$). 

Crucially, propagating an ensemble of networks through non-linear dynamics often yields complex, non-trivial probability distributions for the downstream steady states, making simple point-to-point comparisons insufficient. To properly evaluate these predictions, we employed a Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) log-likelihood metric to measure how well the true perturbed states were supported by the ensemble's predicted probability densities. The necessity of this probabilistic approach was most evident in edge cases such the one depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarking}d. 

For this downstream target, the MAP point-estimate erroneously predicted almost no regulatory change ($\Delta \approx -0.13$). However, the true biological response was a massive down-regulation ($\Delta \approx -1.01$). Evaluation of the ensemble's KDE revealed a highly informative, non-trivial probability distribution that explicitly captured this massive down-regulation, placing the true state in a region of high predictive likelihood. This demonstrates that point-estimates can fail catastrophically in downstream predictions, and that resolving and propagating structural uncertainty is critical for reliable perturbation forecasting in biological networks.

\section{Conclusions}

Recovering the causal topology of stochastic, non-linear dynamical systems from sparse observations remains one of the most mathematically demanding inverse problems in complex systems. In this work, we introduced a fully Bayesian, ensemble-based framework that integrates a dynamical SDE likelihood with a non-parametric Minimum Description Length (MDL) structural prior and a Stochastic Block Model (SBM).

Our results highlight three fundamental principles for robust network inference. First, we demonstrated that overparameterization of intrinsic node dynamics leads to severe structural degeneracy; globally constraining kinetic parameters is essential to force the inference engine to recover genuine network topology rather than absorbing variance through localized kinetic overfitting. Second, we showed that inference algorithms must exploit non-equilibrium, transient trajectories, as steady-state data lacks the necessary causal information to reliably reconstruct directed interactions. Finally, and most importantly, we proved that relying on a single Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) point-estimate is highly brittle for downstream biological applications. By marginalizing over topological uncertainty, our posterior ensemble accurately forecasted the systemic effects of gene knockouts, capturing non-trivial probability distributions and massive regulatory shifts that point-estimates entirely missed.

It is important to note that the very constraint making this inference possible—globally shared kinetic parameters—establishes a fundamental boundary condition for the methodology. As demonstrated in our supplementary application to the highly heterogeneous DREAM4 \textit{in silico} benchmark (see Supplementary Materials), applying a homogeneous global parameter constraint to a system governed by heavily divergent, node-specific thermodynamic rates leads to a model mismatch and degrades structural recovery. Consequently, this approach is best suited for scenarios where the primary variation in node dynamics is driven by network topology rather than massive intrinsic kinetic heterogeneities. 

While this framework was benchmarked in the context of Gene Regulatory Networks, the mathematical foundation—combining continuous SDE likelihoods with discrete block-coordinate MCMC sampling—is entirely general. Future work will focus on scaling the algorithm to massive single-cell transcriptomic datasets (scRNA-seq) and incorporating pseudo-time trajectory ordering to infer *in vivo* developmental networks. Ultimately, embracing and propagating structural uncertainty offers a principled path toward predictive and interpretable models in systems biology.


\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Synthetic network generation and SDE integration}
Ground truth networks of sizes $N \in \{10, 20, 50, 100\}$ were generated as directed scale-free graphs using the Price model ($m=1$) to mimic the presence of biological master regulators. The continuous time-series trajectories were generated by numerically integrating the stochastic differential equation (SDE) model. The wild-type global kinetic parameters were set to a basal degradation rate $\gamma = 0.5$, a maximum coupling strength $V = 2.0$, and an intrinsic noise level $D = 0.02$. The integration was performed using the Euler-Maruyama scheme with a highly resolved internal time-step of $\delta t = 0.01$.

To construct the training datasets, the trajectories were sub-sampled to simulate experimental measurement intervals $\Delta t \in \{0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0\}$. Furthermore, data pairs were extracted from two distinct temporal regimes to evaluate the impact of non-equilibrium dynamics: a highly transient phase (sampling window $t \in [0, 3]$) and a stationary phase (sampling window $t \in [10, 15]$). The number of sampled state-transition pairs $M_{pairs}$ was systematically varied between 100 and 1000. 

\subsection{Bayesian inference and MCMC sampling}
The non-parametric Minimum Description Length (MDL) prior and the Stochastic Block Model (SBM) degree-corrected components were implemented via the \textit{graph-tool} Python library. The inference algorithm was initialized with an empty graph supplemented by a $5\%$ density of random edges to prevent the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampler from trapping in trivial empty-network local minima.

The structural topology was sampled via the $NN_{Descent}$ block-coordinate sweeps, while the continuous global kinetic parameters $\theta = \{\gamma, V, D\}$ were optimized deterministically conditioned on the topology using the Nelder-Mead simplex algorithm. The inference pipeline consisted of 50 burn-in sweeps to allow the algorithm to converge near the global optimum, followed by 100 posterior sampling sweeps from which the probabilistic ensemble of networks was extracted.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
The probabilistic ensemble generated by the MCMC sampler was evaluated using a combination of topological and hierarchical metrics. The ensemble networks were marginalized to compute individual edge probabilities. These probabilities were used as continuous confidence scores to compute the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC) and F1-scores against the binary ground-truth adjacency matrix. 

Due to inherent parameter non-identifiability, evaluating the absolute magnitudes of the inferred regulatory weights is biologically less informative than evaluating the relative regulatory hierarchy. Therefore, the Rank-Biased Overlap (RBO) and Spearman Rank correlation were used to compare the hierarchical ordering of the inferred master-regulators against the true network. For perturbation analyses, the systemic shift in steady states was evaluated using a Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) log-likelihood metric, which measures the probability density assigned by the predicted ensemble to the true perturbed biological state.

\subsection{Benchmarking baselines}
To benchmark against canonical inference paradigms, four widely used methods were employed on the same synthetic datasets: dynGENIE3 (non-parametric Random Forest inference adapted for time-series), Sparse ODE via Lasso (parametric linear regression with L1 regularization cross-validated for sparsity), PySINDy (data-driven sequential thresholded least squares using a polynomial library), and Granger Causality (classical vector autoregression). Hyperparameters for these baselines were selected according to standard default recommendations for biological time-series.

\subsection{In silico perturbation experiments}
To simulate a gene knockout experiment, the true and inferred networks were subjected to an \textit{in silico} perturbation where the target node's expression state and outgoing regulatory weights were permanently forced to zero. The networks were then allowed to relax to their new steady-state distribution. Predictive generalization was evaluated by comparing the continuous probability density of the perturbed steady states marginalized over the entire inferred posterior ensemble against the deterministic prediction of the single Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) point-estimate.
